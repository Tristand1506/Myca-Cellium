# Myca --- Locomotion Reference

Myca's locomotion is a separate biological system from her general
character design. Her legs remain clean, continuous pointed structures
when lifted. Temporary external hyphae appear only after contact with a
natural surface.

## Canonical Leg Shape

-   Preserve Myca's established proportions: recognizable upper legs,
    slender calves, then a long continuous taper from roughly mid-calf
    downward.
-   Each leg terminates in **one clean central point**.
-   No ankle, heel, arch, sole, toes, root-foot, or permanent tendrils.
-   When lifted, nothing protrudes from the point.
-   The lower leg should read as a smooth pale mould-green fungal spike
    rather than a leg standing on tiptoe.

## Contact Mechanism

The pointed leg and the external gripping hyphae are distinct
structures.

### 1. Approach

The clean point approaches the surface. No external hyphae are visible.

### 2. Touch

The single intact point contacts or presses slightly into the substrate.
Its silhouette does not change.

### 3. Sprout

Only after contact, extremely fine hyphae emerge from tiny areas around
the **lower sides of the leg immediately above the contact point**. They
do not replace or divide the terminal point.

### 4. Explore

The new filaments spread outward and downward, repeatedly dividing into
finer branches. Each strand responds independently to nearby terrain.

Hyphae may slip beneath leaf litter, weave through moss, enter loose
soil, follow bark grooves, curl around small stones or twigs, or enter
decomposing material.

### 5. Anchor

The resulting network gently grips the environment while Myca transfers
her weight. The original pointed leg remains clearly visible at the
centre of the contact zone.

### 6. Release

As the leg lifts, the temporary network retracts, detaches, or separates
at microscopic scale. The lifted leg returns to one perfectly clean
point. Only negligible traces may remain in the substrate.

**Movement cycle:** approach → touch → sprout → explore → anchor →
transfer → release.

## Hyphal Appearance

External hyphae should resemble true fungal mycelium enlarged enough to
perceive:

-   extremely fine and hairlike;
-   pale and softly translucent;
-   irregular in thickness and direction;
-   organically curved;
-   increasingly fine with each generation of branching;
-   asymmetric and responsive to terrain;
-   tangled, overlapping, and partly obscured by soil, moss, bark, and
    leaves;
-   mostly illuminated by ambient or transmitted light rather than
    strong bioluminescence.

Avoid plant-root anatomy, thick tendrils, radial starbursts, geometric
branching, bright intersection nodes, constellation-like networks, and
permanent root-feet.

The desired image is **one intact fungal point temporarily fastening
itself to its surroundings with a cloud of microscopic exploratory
filaments**.

## Animation / Reference-Sheet Rule

When illustrating the step cycle, show the **same leg, same camera
angle, and same patch of terrain** throughout the sequence. This makes
the transformation readable as temporary growth rather than six
unrelated leg designs.

## Environmental Behaviour

The network changes according to the substrate:

-   **Forest soil:** enters loose earth and humus.
-   **Moss:** creates a dense shallow weave around stems.
-   **Wood or bark:** follows grooves and curls around fibres.
-   **Stone:** seeks cracks, edges, and surrounding debris.
-   **Leaf litter:** slips beneath and between leaves with relatively
    light growth.

Myca's movement should feel practiced, graceful, adaptive, and quiet.
She does not walk on permanent roots. **For the brief duration of each
step, she establishes a living fungal connection with the surface
supporting her.**
