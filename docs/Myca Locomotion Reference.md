# Myca | Locomotion Reference

**Baseline 1.0 · 8 October 2026 · Behaviour specification, separate from character appearance**

## Purpose and scope

Myca makes temporary mycelial contact with the substrate supporting each step. This is an established fictional movement mechanism, not a claim about real fungal growth speed or load-bearing ability. Her permanent lower-leg geometry is defined in the [Character Design Brief](Myca%20Character%20Design%20Brief.md). This page defines what changes when that leg contacts a surface.

The main visual distinction is **one intact pale-green point plus many much finer temporary filaments**. The point does not split into the filaments, and the filaments do not form a permanent foot.

## Permanent geometry

Retain the long narrow lower leg, very little calf bulk and one uninterrupted terminal taper. The same geometry must persist through approach, contact, gripping and release. It must not acquire a heel, ankle-shaped bend, flattened underside, forward-pointing toe or wider base under load. The lifted leg ends in a clean point without trailing growth.

## Contact-zone origin

New hyphae emerge only **after physical contact**, from a narrow region on the lower sides of the leg immediately above the substrate contact point. They do not replace the terminal tip and do not sprout from a broad area halfway up the shin. The contact zone does not require a raised collar, visible ring or new body part.

The single point remains intact beneath this lateral emergence zone. It can press slightly into a soft substrate without changing its silhouette. The exact penetration depth has not been specified.

## Six-stage step cycle

| Stage | Required visible state | Continuity check |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Approach | The clean point is above the surface; no external hyphae. | The complete point is visible and not foot-shaped. |
| 2. Touch | The point meets the surface; no structural change. | It is the same point, not a substituted root structure. |
| 3. Sprout | A few very fine filaments emerge laterally immediately above contact, growing outward and downward. | The tip stays intact; origins remain close to the substrate. |
| 4. Explore | Filaments divide into increasingly fine branches and follow nearby contours. | The pattern is asymmetric and terrain-dependent. |
| 5. Anchor | A local network grips the substrate while Myca transfers weight. | The point remains legible; strands stay far finer than the leg. |
| 6. Release | The temporary attachment lets go and the leg lifts. The completed airborne state has no attached strands. | The final leg silhouette matches Approach. |

Weight transfer occurs during anchoring, not as a different leg shape. Earlier documents sometimes list it as a seventh label; the six-stage organisation above keeps that action explicit without introducing a second incompatible cycle.

## Filament structure and environmental contact

External hyphae are very thin, pale, softly translucent and irregularly curved. Their thickness varies subtly and branches become progressively finer. They overlap, turn into crevices and disappear beneath material. Visibility comes from their contrast and material response, not glowing intersection points.

Growth seeks nearby purchase rather than tracing a symmetrical circle. Some strands stay short; others extend further before dividing. Several may follow the same favourable surface, but a perfectly radial starburst, a geometric net or a thick root fan is off-model. Most of the web stays close to the substrate; a few short bridges between raised surfaces do not make it an elevated umbrella.

| Substrate | Established direction | Avoid inferring |
|---|---|---|
| Loose soil / humus | Strands enter gaps and branch partly below the surface. | A large excavated root system or deep permanent planting. |
| Moss | Strands weave among stems and surface growth. | A uniformly glowing moss disc. |
| Bark / wood | Strands follow grooves, fibres and small edges. | Thick woody roots or a fixed circular attachment. |
| Stone / debris | Strands follow edges, cracks and nearby material. | A claim she can penetrate solid stone or cling to any vertical wall. |
| Leaf litter | Strands slip beneath and between leaves. | A web floating above the leaves without touching them. |

## Release and persistence

The sources allow retracting, detaching or microscopic separation as release mechanisms; they do not settle which occurs biologically. Preserve that uncertainty. What is fixed visually is a temporary connection and a clean lifted point.

A few negligible traces can remain in the substrate. Do not leave a permanent web attached to an airborne leg or a dense trail of established roots behind every step. A transitional frame may show attachment giving way, but it must not be confused with the final released state. Exact release kinetics, residue lifetime and growth speed remain open production decisions.

## Acting and animation continuity

Myca is accustomed to this movement. Her weight shifts should read as controlled and deliberate rather than as repeated stumbling on unfamiliar limbs. Knees and upper-leg changes still support crouching and stepping; do not invent an ankle articulation to make a pose work. The cap remains connected to the head through every movement.

For explanatory sequences, hold the camera, scale, leg identity and patch of ground constant. Retain recognisable stones, leaves or bark landmarks between panels. A sequence of differently posed legs on different ground cannot demonstrate the mechanism clearly.

Do not convert the illustrated stages into a mandated frame rate. No cycle duration, acceleration, strength limit, walking speed, jump mechanic or climbing ability has been approved.

## Standalone locomotion module

Show Myca's lower leg as a smooth pale mould-green continuous taper ending in one intact point, with a slim calf and no foot or heel. While lifted it has no external filaments. After the point touches the substrate, extremely fine pale hyphae emerge from the lower sides immediately above contact, not from a split tip or high on the shin. They grow outward and downward, divide irregularly and follow soil, moss, leaves, bark or nearby crevices, forming a small asymmetric temporary grip while the original point remains visible. On release, the web lets go and the airborne leg is clean again. Use the same leg and terrain throughout a sequence. Keep strands much finer than the leg, close to the substrate, without thick roots, bright junctions or a symmetrical starburst.
